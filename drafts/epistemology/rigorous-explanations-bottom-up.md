From the Ground Up: What Makes an Explanation Rigorous?
Introduction:
Have you ever asked a question only to get an “explanation” that left you none the wiser? Perhaps someone defined a radius as “half the diameter” and then defined the diameter as “twice the radius,” trapping you in a loop of definitions. Or maybe you’ve heard a fancy phrase like “opium causes sleep because of its dormitive virtue,” which sounds informative but is essentially just restating that opium makes you sleepy. These are examples of explanations that fail to enlighten – they’re circular or assume what they need to explain. In contrast, rigorous explanations feel satisfying: they build our understanding from the ground up, linking complex ideas to simpler foundational concepts. In this article, we’ll explore what makes an explanation rigorous by examining levels of explanation (bottom-up vs. top-down approaches), why we must avoid circular reasoning and redundancy, and we’ll look at examples across disciplines that illustrate the difference. We’ll also connect these ideas to formal theories from philosophy (like reductionism and parsimony), psychology (how our minds handle explanations), and logic (deductive vs. inductive reasoning), and discuss the implications for communication and science. By the end, you’ll have a clearer sense of how to craft (and recognize) explanations that truly deepen understanding.
1. Levels of Explanation: Bottom-Up vs. Top-Down
At the heart of rigorous explanation is the level at which you explain a concept. Broadly, there are two approaches: bottom-up (building understanding from simpler, lower-level components) and top-down (starting from a higher-level overview or theory and working down to details). While both approaches can be useful, bottom-up explanations are generally more rigorous because they derive complex ideas from more fundamental ones, ensuring nothing is taken for granted. Top-down explanations can provide context, but if used alone they risk glossing over the actual “mechanism” or reason behind something. Let’s unpack these approaches: Bottom-up vs. top-down approaches. Bottom-up explanations start with fundamental building blocks and assemble them into a complex concept (imagine climbing up from “Fundamental Concepts” to a “Complex Phenomenon”). Top-down explanations begin with the big picture or whole system and break it into parts (dropping down from a broad “Whole System” to specific components). Both have their place, but bottom-up tends to ensure each step is grounded in something already understood.
Bottom-Up Explanation: In a bottom-up approach, you explain X by relying on more basic, previously understood concepts below it. You might also hear this called a reductionist explanation – it reduces a phenomenon to its constituents. For example, suppose you want to explain what a cell is. A bottom-up explanation might start with molecules: describe how proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids come together to form organelles, and how those organelles work together as a living cell. Each concept is derived from a lower-level concept: organelles from molecules, molecules from atoms, and so on. This approach is rigorous because it ensures that each piece of the explanation is already either known or defined. You’re not allowed to invoke a mysterious higher-level idea without showing how it arises from simpler parts. As physicist Sabine Hossenfelder puts it, “Reductionism… is the idea that you can understand things by taking them apart into smaller things.” In science, this hypothesis – that the properties of a system are determined by its constituents – has been overwhelmingly supported. When we truly understand something, we can often trace it to deeper principles. In fact, explanations that drill down to lower levels often have more explanatory power, meaning they can do more than just describe the current case – they can predict new outcomes. This predictive power comes from being rooted in fundamental mechanisms, rather than just re-labeling the phenomenon. Bottom-up thinking is “the basic way physicists think: lower level action underlies higher level behavior”. It’s considered rigorous because it leaves little unexplained – it keeps digging until it reaches bedrock.
Top-Down Explanation: A top-down explanation does the reverse – it starts from a broad concept, category, or theory and then explains how X fits into it or follows from it. This can be useful for context or for understanding the purpose or function of something. For example, if explaining a cell top-down, one might start by saying “a cell is the basic functional unit of life, forming the building block of tissues and organs (the bigger picture), and our cells work together to keep us alive.” Then one might mention a few internal parts of the cell as needed. Top-down explanations often answer “why is this important or what role does it play?” before they answer “how does it work?”. They can be teleological – explaining things in terms of goals or purposes. Consider explaining why an airplane is flying. A top-down answer could be: “It flies because it was designed to fly – engineers constructed it with wings, engines, and a shape intended for flight, within the context of our society’s need for air transport.” This gives a big-picture reason (the design intent and purpose) but doesn’t yet tell you how the plane stays in the air. Top-down explanations can sometimes sound like “It’s part of a grand plan” or “It fits into a known theory.” They are viable when the higher-level framework is well-understood by the audience (so they can see how the specific thing follows), or when you specifically want to emphasize meaning and context over mechanism. However, used alone, top-down explanations can be too abstract. They might assume knowledge of the overarching theory or simply rename the mystery. In the worst case, a top-down explanation degenerates into circularity (“X happens because that’s just the kind of thing that happens” – which explains nothing new). In our airplane example, saying “it flies because it was designed to” doesn’t tell us what physical forces keep it aloft – for that we’d need to invoke a bottom-up explanation (like air pressure differences and physics of lift). In short, top-down gives a birds-eye view and bottom-up gives a ground-level view. Truly rigorous explanations often combine both: they use bottom-up mechanics to avoid mystery and top-down context to avoid getting lost in details. A classic paper on complex systems notes that higher-level (top-down) explanations “rely on the existence of the lower level mechanisms in order to succeed”. Without the solid foundation of bottom-up detail, a top-down story can’t actually cause anything – it’s the lower-level pieces doing the work.
Why Bottom-Up Is Usually More Rigorous: Bottom-up explanations force us to confront the actual cause-and-effect and derivation of a concept. They reduce the chance of hidden assumptions or logical gaps. If someone explains X by referencing Y, and Y is simpler or more fundamental, we can then ask “and what explains Y?” – if that too can be explained by Z, even more fundamental, and so on, eventually we reach something well-known or accepted. This stepwise grounding is the opposite of taking a shortcut. By contrast, a purely top-down explanation might start from a bird’s-eye principle that the audience doesn’t actually understand fully, or it might just label the phenomenon without dissecting it. Bottom-up = fewer leaps of faith. It aligns with the scientific spirit of reductionism: complex phenomena should be explained in the simplest terms possible. This principle of parsimony (related to Occam’s razor) says that a good explanation doesn’t introduce more assumptions than necessary. Bottom-up often naturally follows parsimony by trying to use only well-established building blocks. Top-down, if misapplied, can violate parsimony by invoking big, vague concepts that raise more questions. To illustrate the contrast, imagine explaining a simple household phenomenon like why a refrigerator keeps food cold. A top-down explanation might be: “Because it’s a refrigerator – it’s designed to keep things cold. You put food in, and it stays cold due to the refrigeration cycle.” This points to a general idea (refrigerators refrigerate) but doesn’t yet explain how. A bottom-up explanation would start from the mechanism: “Inside the fridge is a compressor that pushes a refrigerant fluid through coils. As the fluid expands, it absorbs heat from inside the fridge, carrying that heat to the coils at the back and releasing it outside. This heat-exchange cycle is driven by the compressor motor, which is why you sometimes hear it humming.” Now you’ve broken it down into parts: fluid, compressor, coils, heat absorption and release – each of which is a more basic concept in physics or engineering. You could even go further down (why does expanding fluid absorb heat? etc.), but you see how bottom-up gave a mechanistic chain of reasoning. It’s harder to argue with or be confused by such an explanation because each piece gives new information. In fact, a bottom-up explanation often predicts or at least is consistent with observable details (like: “feel the warm coils at the back – that’s the heat being released”). A top-down explanation (“it works because it’s designed to”) might be true but isn’t illuminating. We generally consider an explanation rigorous when it illuminates the underlying process rather than just citing a label or higher authority. In sum, bottom-up explanations tend to be more rigorous because they closely follow logic and causation. They mirror deductive reasoning in many cases: starting from known principles and deducing the phenomenon (e.g. using physics laws to deduce why the fridge cools). Top-down explanations are more akin to inductive reasoning or even abductive reasoning: starting from an observation or high-level pattern and fitting it into a framework. That can be helpful for discovery or intuition, but until you break it down (deduce it) you haven’t fully proven or explained it. As a rule of thumb, if an explanation can be visualized as a tower of blocks, a rigorous one builds from the bottom – ensuring a solid base – rather than hanging something heavy from a skyhook at the top with no support beneath. In the next section, we’ll see how explanations fail when they don’t follow this principle, leading to circular reasoning and redundancy.
2. Avoiding Circular Reasoning and Redundancy
One of the quickest ways an explanation loses rigor is by falling into a circular reasoning trap or by being redundant. A circular explanation, sometimes called a vacuous explanation, is one that, in effect, explains nothing at all – it just repeats the question or phenomenon in different words. It’s like saying “I’m hungry because I need food” – you haven’t given a real reason; you’ve just restated the problem. Rigorous explanations scrupulously avoid this. What is Circular Reasoning in Explanations?
In logic, circular reasoning (also known as begging the question) means your conclusion is hidden in your premises. In explanations, a circular explanation means the thing to be explained is used as part of its own explanation. In other words, it provides no new information – it might as well say “X is true because X is true.” A classic (and comical) example originates from Molière’s play The Imaginary Invalid: a doctor “explains” that opium causes people to fall asleep because it possesses a “dormitive virtue” (Latin for “sleep-inducing power”). This sounds learned, but it’s completely circular – why does opium induce sleep? Saying “because it has a sleep-inducing power” is just a fancy tautology; opium’s ability to cause sleep is being explained by… opium’s ability to cause sleep! Such an explanation is vacuous – it’s essentially the event itself posing as an explanation. Everyday Circular Explanations: You don’t need to know Latin to encounter circular explanations; they crop up in everyday life and even in dictionaries. For example, imagine a child asks: “Why does the Earth orbit the Sun?” If one replies, “Because of gravity,” that’s fine – but if the child then asks “What is gravity?” and the reply is “It’s the force that makes the Earth orbit the Sun,” we have a problem. We’ve defined gravity by the very phenomenon (orbiting) we were asked to explain – a circular loop. Without an independent description of gravity (e.g. mass attracting mass, as quantified by Newton’s law), “because of gravity” is just a label, not an explanation. Similarly, consider trying to define two geometric terms for someone: Circular definition between radius and diameter. In this diagram, the concept of “Radius” and “Diameter” are pointing to each other in a loop. If one defines radius as “half of the diameter” and diameter as “twice the radius,” neither term gets an independent explanation – they just send you round in circles. This kind of definition pair is mutually circular and unhelpful to someone who doesn’t already know one of the terms. A rigorous approach would break the cycle: for instance, define radius in a bottom-up way (“the radius is the distance from the center of a circle to its edge”), which doesn’t assume knowledge of diameter. Then you can define diameter as “twice the radius,” which is now meaningful because radius was given a real meaning. The key is that at least one term gets a fundamental definition grounded in something more basic (distance in this case), instead of each just referencing the other. We also often see circularity in dictionary definitions or thesaurus entries. Have you ever chased a word through a dictionary, only to find it defined by synonyms that eventually lead back to the original word? For example, suppose “oak” is defined as “a type of tree that grows from an acorn,” and then “acorn” is defined as “the nut produced by an oak tree.” If you start with no idea what an oak or acorn is, these two definitions just send you to each other and provide no new information. (In fact, this is a real noted example of a circular definition in lexicography.) Likewise, defining “cell phone” as “a phone that is cellular” is a redundant tautology that doesn’t tell the learner what “cellular” means in this context. Rigorous reference works work hard to avoid such loops. As one linguistics text notes, a definition is deficient if the audience must already know the term being defined – authors should strive to provide new, useful information in a definition. Signs of Circular or Redundant Explanations:
Rephrasing the question as the answer: If someone asks “what is procrastination?” and you answer “it’s the act of procrastinating” or “it’s when someone is procrastinative,” you’ve given them zero new information (just changed the word form). This is essentially circular. A non-circular explanation would be “procrastination is delaying or postponing something you need to do, often against your better judgment” – notice this explains the concept without using the word procrastinate itself.
Using synonyms that assume the same knowledge: e.g., “What’s an emperor?” – “A ruler.” If the person doesn’t know what ruler means in that context (they might think of a measuring tool!), this is not a complete explanation. Ideally, you’d say “An emperor is a kind of ruler – meaning, a person who rules over an empire, which is a large group of territories or countries.” That adds detail beyond a one-word synonym.
Infinite regress of definitions: Sometimes a circular explanation isn’t a simple A→A loop, but a chain that eventually loops back. Imagine: A is explained by B, B by C, and C by A again. This can happen subtly with abstract concepts. For example, defining “justice” as “fairness,” “fairness” as “impartiality,” and then “impartiality” as “acting with justice” – at some point, the chain closes in on itself. A rigorous approach would break out of the loop by connecting at least one of those terms to a concrete, lower-level idea or observable criterion.
It’s worth noting that not all cycles in explanation are viciously circular. Sometimes an explanation can involve feedback loops or mutual influence that are real (especially in complex systems). The danger is when the explanation doesn’t go anywhere new. If an event is “explained” purely by itself, we’ve accomplished nothing. The psychologist Frank Keil and colleagues point out that even children as young as five show a preference for non-circular explanations, and by age ten kids robustly reject circular answers. For instance, if you tell a child “This bird has wings because it’s a bird that can fly,” they might sense that’s not satisfying – you haven’t actually explained how or why wings help it fly, you’ve just stated something tautological. Children quickly learn that a good explanation teaches them something new. As we get older, we too should hold our explanations to that standard. Redundancy vs. Rigor:
Redundancy in explanation is closely related to circularity. A redundant explanation might not be strictly circular, but it wastes time on information that doesn’t increase understanding. For example, someone asks “Why did the circuit short-circuit?” and the answer is, “Because it experienced a short.” That’s basically using the same word in noun and verb form – redundant and unhelpful. Another example: “This painting is popular because many people like it.” That’s a trivial truth (what does “popular” mean if not that people like it?) and doesn’t actually reveal underlying causes (e.g. why do people like it?). A rigorous explanation tries to minimize redundancy and maximize informative content. It might repeat certain terms for clarity, but it ensures that each part of the explanation adds something. Sometimes, circular or redundant answers are given jokingly or dismissively – like a parent might answer a persistent “why” from a child with “Because I said so.” In effect, “because I say so” is a top-down appeal to authority that avoids giving a real reason. It’s not circular in the logical sense, but it’s certainly not a bottom-up or illuminating explanation either. In science or serious discourse, falling back on authority or definition without deeper reasoning is similarly frowned upon. We want explanations that break out of loops and dive into the real details. In the next section, we’ll tour through several disciplines and scenarios to see concrete examples of explanations done poorly (circular or superficial) versus done rigorously (bottom-up and illuminating). This will reinforce the concepts and show how they apply universally – in science, philosophy, education, and everyday life.
3. Examples Across Disciplines: Circular vs. Bottom-Up Explanations
To drive home the differences between flimsy and rigorous explanations, let’s look at examples in various domains. For each example, we’ll consider what a circular or non-explanatory attempt might look like, and then what a good bottom-up explanation would be. We’ll also note if a top-down angle adds anything, or if it’s viable at all. By analyzing why each explanation succeeds or fails, we can recognize patterns that apply anywhere.
Example 1: Science – Opium’s “Dormitive Virtue”
Circular Explanation: “Opium causes people to fall asleep because it has a dormitive virtue (a sleep-inducing power).” This was Molière’s satirical example of a bogus scientific explanation. Here the explanandum (thing to explain) is opium’s sleep effect. The explanans (purported explanation) is just a Latinized restatement of that effect. It’s circular because knowing it has a “sleep-inducing power” is no different from knowing it causes sleep. This is a prime example of labeling a phenomenon instead of explaining it. It’s redundant and adds no insight into how opium actually works on the body.
Bottom-Up Explanation: A rigorous, bottom-up explanation of opium’s effect would dig into pharmacology. For instance: “Opium (and its derivatives like morphine) contain compounds that bind to specific receptors in the brain and nervous system (opioid receptors). When these receptors are activated, they trigger biochemical changes that slow down neural activity and produce sedation. In essence, opium’s chemicals mimic natural neurotransmitters that reduce pain and induce sleep, which is why taking opium makes you drowsy.” Notice how this explanation goes to a lower level of biology and chemistry – receptors, neural activity – rather than just restating “it causes sleep because it causes sleep.” It introduces causal mechanisms: binding to receptors causes neural inhibition, which causes sedation. This explanation is more complex, but it’s rigorous because each step can be further examined or is based on known biology. If you wanted, you could go even further (why do those receptors cause neural slowdown? what are neurotransmitters? etc.), but even at this level, we’ve broken the mystery into smaller pieces. We haven’t assumed “sleepiness power” as a given; we’ve derived it from molecular interactions. This bottom-up answer also has predictive power: it implies that anything else that binds those receptors similarly will also cause sleepiness – a testable claim, and indeed how pharmacologists discovered other sedatives.
Top-Down Angle: Is a top-down explanation viable here? Perhaps to give historical or functional context: “Opium has been used as a sedative for centuries; it’s essentially a potent drug that depresses the central nervous system.” This places opium’s effect in a broader category (depressants) and historical usage, which is informative background. But notice, it still doesn’t explain the mechanism (it basically says “it’s a sedative because it’s a depressant,” which is a bit circular unless you know what “depressant” means mechanistically). Top-down context (e.g. classification of opium as an opioid, a class of drugs known to relieve pain and cause sleep) can help someone connect opium’s effect to a bigger picture of drug types. However, without at least some mechanistic explanation, it’s not fully rigorous. In scientific explanation, top-down is often just categorization. Useful, but secondary to the bottom-up cause-effect chain.
Why the Circular Explanation Fails: It fails because it stops at the surface. It’s essentially saying “opium causes sleep because it causes sleep” – just swapping in a synonymous phrase. It leaves the curious mind unsatisfied (we could ask “but why does it have a dormitive virtue? what gives it that power?” and the circular explanation has no answer). The bottom-up one succeeds because it goes deeper: it links the phenomenon to lower-level concepts (chemistry and physiology) that are independently understood. Each piece of that chain can be examined or verified, making the explanation robust. If you remove any piece (say, if opium didn’t bind those receptors), the explanation would break – which means each piece is doing real explanatory work, not just dressing up the conclusion.
Example 2: Physics – Defining Circular Motion (Radius and Diameter)
We touched on this earlier with the radius and diameter example, but let’s lay it out clearly:
Circular Explanation: “A radius is half of a diameter; a diameter is two times the radius.” If someone only knows general English and hears this, they effectively learn nothing new about what a radius or diameter actually is. Each term is defined only in terms of the other. This is a classic circular definition. It’s a closed loop that a newcomer can’t escape. The explanation is redundant too: saying “radius is half the diameter” and “diameter is twice the radius” are actually the same statement mathematically, just inverted. So it’s basically one piece of information being passed back and forth, but neither term gets grounded in a more fundamental concept (like distance or geometry fundamentals).
Bottom-Up Explanation: One rigorous way is: “Imagine a circle (like a perfect round disk). Draw a line straight from the center of the circle to its edge – that line is a radius. Now, if you draw a line all the way across the circle, passing through the center from one side to the other, that full line is a diameter. Naturally, that full line is made of two radius lengths back-to-back, so the diameter is twice the radius.” This explanation first defines radius in an atomic way – it doesn’t assume you know diameter; it uses everyday spatial ideas (center, edge of a circle). It then defines diameter using the already-defined radius. This is bottom-up in the sense that it started with the simpler concept (radius can be visualized easily as a single line from center to circumference) and then built up to a larger concept (diameter as two radii). Alternatively, one could start with diameter (perhaps one could argue diameter is simpler – “the longest distance across a circle”) and then define radius as half of that. Either way, the key is: don’t trap the definition in a loop. Define one in terms of basic concepts, then relate the other to it. The result is non-circular and clear.
Top-Down Explanation: In geometry, a top-down explanation might not apply strongly for a single term like radius. However, one could give context: “Circles have several important measures – the radius is one of them. If you know the radius, you can get the diameter by doubling it, and you can get the circumference by a formula, etc.” This places radius in a broader framework of circle properties (where radius, diameter, circumference, area are all interrelated). It’s useful to know the big picture (e.g. knowing diameters, radii, circumferences are all connected by π, etc.), but again, until radius or diameter is defined in a non-circular way, those relationships hang in mid-air. So top-down context here is only helpful after bottom-up definitions.
Why Circular Fails: It fails because it violates a basic rule of definitions: do not use the term (or its direct relatives) in its own definition. A rigorous definition or explanation breaks out of that self-reference. The bottom-up version succeeded by appealing to an intuitive concept (drawing a line in a circle). It showed what these terms mean, rather than just telling their interrelationship. Once you have that, explaining that one is twice the other actually becomes meaningful knowledge rather than a tautology. In general, in mathematics and science, we take great care to define foundational terms without circularity – otherwise the whole logical structure can crumble. If you ever open a math textbook, you’ll see a progression: some primitive concepts are stated (sometimes even without definition, treated as intuitive or axiomatic), then other concepts are defined in terms of those. If at any point you loop back (define A by B and B by A without independent basis), you’ve introduced a hole in the logic. Rigorous explanation is like constructing a building with solid supports, not an unsupported arch where each piece leans on the other with nothing holding them up.
Example 3: Philosophy – What is Virtue?
Philosophical concepts are notoriously tricky to define and explain without circularity. Plato, in his dialogue Meno, explored the question “What is virtue?” and deliberately demonstrated the pitfalls of circular definition.
Circular Explanation (Failed Attempt in Dialogue): One definition proposed was “Virtue is the power of attaining good, justly.” On the surface that might sound plausible. But Plato (through Socrates) points out a problem: in that very definition, the word “justly” invokes the concept of justice, which itself is usually considered a part of virtue (justice is one of the virtues). In essence, the definition used a part of the concept to define the whole – virtue was defined in terms of justice, which is a kind of virtue. This is a form of circularity (not as blatant as some examples, but still problematic). It’s saying, “one type of virtue (justice) is used to explain virtue.” Socrates rightly notes they have to start over, because they haven’t actually nailed down what virtue itself is without presupposing it in the explanation. This subtle circularity can happen whenever we use closely related terms that essentially reference the same concept. If someone asked “What is consciousness?” and you answered “it’s the state of being conscious of something,” you’ve similarly defined a word by using its own derivative.
Bottom-Up Explanation: Defining abstract concepts like virtue bottom-up is challenging – whole philosophical treatises are devoted to it. But a rigorous approach tries to break the concept into more fundamental attributes or examples that don’t assume the concept. For virtue, one might explain it as: “Virtue is a trait or quality that is considered morally good. To explain from the ground up, think of concrete virtues like honesty, kindness, courage – each of these is a pattern of behavior or a disposition to act in a way that society or reason deems ethically positive. Virtue, in general, is what all those good traits have in common: they contribute to moral excellence. For instance, honesty is a virtue because it leads one to tell the truth, which builds trust and is morally right; courage is a virtue because it allows one to do the right thing even when scared. So virtue can be understood by looking at such examples and seeing that a virtue consistently benefits others or oneself in ethical ways and is part of good character.” This explanation doesn’t use the word “virtue” in defining itself; instead it derives the concept from examples (honesty, courage, etc.) which are more concrete. It’s somewhat inductive (listing particulars to explain the general), but that’s often how we have to explain big abstractions – by reference to relatable ideas or experiences. Another angle: one could reduce it to more fundamental philosophical ideas, like “virtue is a disposition aligned with the good or human flourishing” – but then “good” would need explanation if not obvious. No matter what, you see the effort: avoid a self-referential loop (like “virtue is acting virtuously” – a non-explanation) and give building blocks.
Top-Down Explanation: In philosophy, a top-down explanation might put virtue in a larger framework. For example, “According to Plato, virtue is an ideal Form – an ultimate quality that all particular virtues partake in. In Christian philosophy, virtue comes from aligning with God’s will. In Aristotle, virtues are the midpoint between extremes (the golden mean).” These are high-level paradigms explaining virtue in terms of even higher metaphysical or theological concepts. They can deepen appreciation for what virtue means in different contexts, but for a newcomer asking “what is virtue?” these can be overwhelming or assume belief in those frameworks. A top-down approach works if the person already buys into that broader theory (Plato’s Forms, etc.), but if not, it might be like using one mystery to explain another. Philosophy often oscillates between top-down (starting from a grand theory of the good) and bottom-up (starting from observations of human behavior). A rigorous philosophical explanation tries to be clear about its foundations – if it’s top-down, it will define or justify those higher principles first; if bottom-up, it will use empirical or common notions to build up.
Why the Failed Attempt Fails: In the Meno case, the attempt failed because it wasn’t truly bottom-up or independent. It tried to define virtue by using a component of virtue (justice), which is as good as using virtue itself. The successful approach in explanation tends to be either analytical (break the concept into more fundamental concepts that don’t implicitly contain it) or example-driven (give specific instances and abstract). Philosophers have identified this issue: a circular definition tells us nothing new. A good explanation in philosophy often requires reducing ambiguity – for instance, defining “justice” without using the word “just” or defining “knowledge” without presupposing what it means “to know.” These are infamously hard tasks, but they force clarity. The process of unpacking a concept into simpler terms is essentially bottom-up: you keep asking “What does that really mean? Can I explain that in plainer language or more fundamental ideas?” – much like a child repeatedly asking “why?” until you reach something basic. As long as you can keep answering in new terms, you’re being rigorous and non-circular.
Example 4: Education & Communication – Teaching a Complex Concept
Imagine a classroom scenario: a student asks, “Why do we see different phases of the Moon?” This is a question about astronomy that can be explained well or poorly.
Poor (Potentially Circular) Explanation: “We see phases of the Moon because the Moon goes through phases.” (Yes, someone might actually say this in a moment of absent-mindedness!) This is tautological – just rephrasing the question as a statement. Slightly better but still problematic: “Because of the way the Sun lights the Moon.” That sounds promising, but if left at that, it’s incomplete enough to be unsatisfying – it labels a cause (sunlight) but doesn’t describe how it leads to phases. Or consider a teacher who says: “It’s just the way the Moon is positioned; sometimes it’s full, sometimes it’s not.” Again, merely restating observations (“full” vs not full) without mechanism.
Rigorous Bottom-Up Explanation: “The Moon doesn’t produce its own light; we see it because it reflects sunlight. As the Moon orbits the Earth, we see varying amounts of its sunlit half. At Full Moon, the Moon is on the opposite side of Earth from the Sun, so we see the entire sunlit face. At New Moon, the Moon is between Earth and the Sun, so the side facing us is dark. The phases in between (crescent, quarter, gibbous) are when we see part of the sunlit side and part of the shadowed side. So the phases are caused by the geometry of the Sun, Earth, and Moon – basically, we’re seeing different fractions of the Moon’s daylight side.” This explanation builds from simpler ideas: (1) the Moon is lit by the Sun, (2) it orbits Earth, (3) depending on angles we see a portion of the lit side, (4) that portion appears as phases. Each of those can be further explained if needed (why the Moon orbits, etc.), but the key is it adds understanding. It’s not a circular statement; it’s a little narrative of cause and effect. It also connects to something the student likely knows (the concept of day and night on Earth – by analogy, the Moon has a day side and night side). This is rigorous in an educational sense: it doesn’t assume you already knew the answer, and it doesn’t use the word “phase” to explain “phases” – it derives phases from orbital mechanics.
Top-Down Explanation: A top-down way to supplement this might be: “Phases of the Moon are a predictable cycle; they’re part of the broader motions of the Earth-Moon-Sun system. Many calendars and cultural myths have developed around them.” This is context and “big picture” but doesn’t explain the cause. A teacher might first say, “The Moon goes through a cycle roughly every 29.5 days – that’s its phases cycle.” That sets the stage (top-down: it’s a repeating pattern on a monthly timescale), but then they must go bottom-up to actually explain why those phases occur. In education, pure top-down often corresponds to just giving a name to a phenomenon or stating a fact (“It’s because of the lunar cycle” – which is basically renaming the phases as “the lunar cycle” without explaining it). So educators know to quickly follow up any top-level description with a breakdown. Cognitive psychology tells us that learners need connections to prior knowledge – a top-down “just so” description can fly over a student’s head, while a bottom-up explanation can hook onto things they already grasp (like the idea of a light source and a sphere).
Why the Rigorous Explanation Succeeds: It gives the mechanism and the model. It doesn’t hide behind jargon or just re-label what’s happening; it shows you why, in terms of simpler ideas (orbit positions, sunlight reflection). Importantly, it’s also not too complex – it’s pitched at the right level. A rigorous explanation to a general audience should still be accessible. If, instead, I answered with an extremely technical bottom-up explanation involving spherical harmonics or albedo or something, it might overshoot what the student can digest. Rigor doesn’t mean unnecessary complexity; it means sound, non-circular reasoning and using appropriate fundamentals. Here, the fundamentals were basic astronomy and geometry, which are appropriate.
This example highlights another aspect: avoiding cognitive overload. If an explanation is too convoluted, it can fail even if it’s technically “bottom-up correct.” A good explainer will introduce concepts stepwise. Educational research (specifically cognitive load theory) suggests that we should break down complex information into simpler parts and build on existing knowledge. This is essentially the pedagogical justification for bottom-up explanations – they manage the learner’s cognitive load by grounding new ideas in familiar ones. A circular explanation, in contrast, often leaves the learner floundering because nothing new was anchored to something known – it was all a mirage.
Example 5: Everyday Conversation – “Just because” vs. Reasoning
Not all explanations deal with science or abstract concepts; sometimes we seek explanations for everyday events or decisions.
Everyday Circularity: Suppose you ask a friend, “Why do you always take the long route home?” If they answer, “Because I prefer it,” and you probe “Why do you prefer it?”, and they say, “Well, it’s my favorite route,” – they’ve basically given you a loop (preference = favorite). You still don’t know why they prefer it. Maybe they haven’t articulated it themselves (could be because it’s more scenic or less traffic, etc.). This is common in everyday explanations – people often give a quick answer that’s effectively restating their stance instead of explaining it. “Why are you going to bed so early?” – “Because I want to get to sleep sooner.” That’s practically restating the conclusion. A more informative answer would be “Because I have to wake up early tomorrow for a meeting, so I need to be rested.”
Better Bottom-Up Answer: If the friend were being rigorous (or just clear), they might say, “I take the long route because even though it’s longer, it’s less busy and more relaxing – I avoid the stress of traffic. I find it’s worth the extra few minutes.” That explanation gives a reason rooted in a lower-level concept: the experience during the drive (traffic vs. relaxation) leads to the choice. It’s not just “I like it because I like it” – it provides an understandable cause (heavy traffic stresses them, so they avoid it).
Top-Down in Everyday Explanations: Sometimes people appeal to a general principle or preference as a kind of top-down explanation: “It’s just my routine” or “I’m just built that way” or “because it’s tradition.” These are broad and can sometimes be conversation-enders. They might be true in a sense (the person has a habit, or a personality quirk, or it’s a tradition), but they often leave a curious person wanting more detail. For instance, “It’s tradition” could lead to “How did the tradition start?” etc. In casual conversation, we often accept these as answers if we don’t need deep rigor, but you can see they are not very explanatory – they’re more like placing the thing into a bucket (“habit,” “tradition”) without explaining the genesis.
Why Clarity Matters: In everyday communication, being rigorous (to a reasonable degree) matters for effective communication and avoiding misunderstandings. If you only give circular or top-down answers, people might think you’re evading the question or that you don’t actually know the reason. In fact, sometimes circular answers hide a lack of understanding – which brings us to an interesting psychological insight: people often think they understand things better than they do, a phenomenon called the illusion of explanatory depth. For example, many of us feel we know how a bicycle works, but when pressed to explain it, we might flounder beyond saying “you pedal and it goes.” That vague understanding feels sufficient until someone asks for a rigorous explanation (gears, balance, friction, etc.), and we realize we had a shallow grasp. The illusion of explanatory depth basically says we mistake familiarity (“I’ve seen bikes, I’ve used one”) for actual understanding of mechanism. It’s only when we attempt a bottom-up explanation that gaps become apparent. So, pushing ourselves to explain things clearly (even everyday decisions) can reveal whether we truly understand our own motives or the phenomena around us. If you find yourself giving a circular answer, it’s a hint that you might not have fully thought through the cause – or you’re not expressing it. Either way, reflecting and then offering a more detailed reason improves communication.
After surveying these examples – from science to philosophy to daily life – the pattern is clear. Bad explanations tend to be circular, tautological, or too high-level, whereas good explanations break things down, often to more concrete or fundamental elements. They avoid assuming the very thing to be explained. They might require a bit more effort or step-by-step reasoning, but they leave the audience with a feeling of “Oh, I get it now.” In the next section, we’ll connect these observations to some formal theories and principles that underlie why bottom-up explanations are valued, and why our brains crave explanations that don’t just talk in circles.
4. Formal Theories on Explanation
Throughout history, philosophers, scientists, and educators have analyzed what counts as a good explanation. Many formal concepts align with the intuition that explanations should be grounded and non-circular. Let’s briefly tour some relevant theories and principles from philosophy, psychology, and logic:
Reductionism vs. Emergentism (Philosophy of Science)
In philosophy, reductionism is the view that a complex system or phenomenon can (in principle) be fully explained by understanding its simpler constituents. A reductionist would say: biology is explained by chemistry, which is explained by physics, and so on. This worldview underpins the bottom-up approach – the belief that lower-level explanations are the most fundamental and rigorous. As one scientist quipped, “there is not a single known example [in nature] where higher-level properties cannot in principle be derived from lower-level ones” (this refers to “weak emergence,” see below). Reductionism has been enormously successful in science – for example, the behavior of gases was explained by the motions of molecules (kinetic theory), genetic inheritance was explained by DNA molecules, and so forth. The strength of reductionism is that it tends to produce deep understanding and predictive power (as Hossenfelder noted, reductionist theories often “lead to new predictions”). When you find the underlying mechanism, you can often manipulate it to achieve new results. It also guards against mystical or circular explanations, because you’re always looking to push deeper into concrete details. Emergentism, on the other hand, emphasizes that sometimes higher-level phenomena have properties that are not obvious from the sum of the parts. An emergent property is one that “comes about from the collective behavior of many constituents”. A classic example: consciousness is often cited as an emergent property of many neurons interacting – even if you know everything about individual neurons, the experience of consciousness might not be straightforward to derive. Another example: the wetness of water is an emergent property – individual H₂O molecules aren’t wet; wetness emerges when many interact. Emergentism doesn’t necessarily deny reductionism; there are two types usually discussed: weak emergence (the higher-level property does ultimately derive from lower levels, but it’s just really complex to do the derivation), and strong emergence (the higher-level property is fundamentally novel and cannot be derived even in principle from below). Most scientists accept weak emergence (complex patterns that are hard to calculate from initial parts, like weather patterns or ant colony behavior) but are skeptical of strong emergence, since no clear example has been found in nature. How does this relate to rigorous explanation? A reductionist mindset encourages bottom-up explanations: explain the higher in terms of the lower. Emergentism reminds us that sometimes explaining the higher requires understanding collective effects or new principles that appear when parts interact. However, even emergent properties are usually explained by showing how they emerge from lower levels (which is again a bottom-up exercise, just a challenging one). Emergentism also supports a multi-level approach to explanation: acknowledging that different levels of description (micro vs. macro) can each provide legitimate insights. For example, to explain an airplane flying, one can give a reductionist physical explanation (Bernoulli’s principle, thrust and lift forces – bottom-up) and a higher-level purposeful explanation (it flies because it was engineered to fulfill a transportation function – top-down). Both are true, addressing different aspects. A fully rigorous explanation might indeed incorporate multiple levels – but it will be careful not to let the higher level just rest on itself. It will link levels. In science, when someone says a phenomenon is emergent, it is not carte blanche to hand-wave; it usually means “we haven’t yet worked out the detailed reductionist chain, but we know it somehow arises from these interactions.” The work remains to fill in the bottom-up story as far as possible. And often, we do manage it: emergent properties like pressure, temperature, and phase transitions have been explained in terms of atomic behavior, just with statistical methods. In summary, reductionism underpins the push for bottom-up rigor, while emergentism cautions that some explanations might need to reference higher-level patterns (especially for complexity). But even then, clarity about how those patterns relate to constituents keeps us from circular reasoning. A reductionist would warn: if you’re saying “the system does this because of emergent property Y,” be careful that “emergent” isn’t just a placeholder word like “dormitive virtue” – ideally, show how Y emerges.
The Principle of Parsimony (Occam’s Razor)
Often paraphrased as “the simplest explanation is usually the best,” Occam’s Razor is the idea that among competing explanations that all fit the facts, the one with the fewest assumptions or entities is preferred. How does parsimony tie in? A circular explanation might seem simple (“opium causes sleep because it does”), but it’s actually empty, not truly an explanation. A rigorous explanation might introduce more moving parts (receptors, neurons, etc. in our opium example), which on the face of it is more complex. Does that violate Occam’s razor? Not really – Occam’s razor is about not multiplying assumptions beyond necessity. A circular explanation often implicitly assumes what it’s supposed to explain (which is one big unnecessary assumption!). A proper explanation might have more detail, but those details are independently evidenced pieces rather than new unexplained mysteries. Occam’s razor encourages us to not invoke superfluous or higher-level entities if we don’t need to. For example, if you can explain a disease by known biological processes, you shouldn’t also invoke, say, an astrological cause – that would be adding an unnecessary assumption. In terms of levels, Occam’s razor would nudge us toward lower-level explanations when they suffice, rather than postulating a brand new principle for each phenomenon. This resonates with bottom-up rigor. However, Occam’s razor doesn’t mean “explain everything in terms of quarks and electrons” for everyday questions – that would violate necessity of a different kind (it would overwhelm and not actually clarify the specific phenomenon at our human scale). Instead, it means don’t make your explanation more complicated than needed to account for what’s being explained. A redundant explanation often fails this: if you basically say the same thing twice in different words (“the car won’t start because it’s not starting”), you haven’t simplified anything – you’ve just tautologically restated it. A rigorous explanation might say “the car won’t start because the battery is dead.” That’s a simpler cause than, say, “the car won’t start because invisible gremlins are tampering with it” – the latter adds an entire imaginary entity. Good explanations often have a certain elegance: they make you think, “Oh, that makes sense without forcing me to buy into something wild or unknown.” Parsimony is about that elegance. In science, parsimony often guides theory choice. If Theory A explains 10 phenomena with 2 assumptions, and Theory B explains the same 10 phenomena with 5 assumptions, A is considered more parsimonious (and likely more rigorous) – provided both truly explain those phenomena. A circular explanation might claim zero assumptions (“it just is”), but in truth it explains 0 phenomena, so it doesn’t even qualify in the race. Parsimony is a reminder: explain as much as possible with as little as possible, and ensure those “little” are well-founded. Bottom-up doing more with less can achieve that, because fundamental principles (like Newton’s laws) can explain a wide range of things without having to invent a new rule for every observation.
Deductive vs. Inductive Reasoning (Logic)
When we think about explanation, we should also clarify the reasoning style: deductive reasoning draws specific conclusions from general rules, whereas inductive reasoning draws general conclusions from specific examples. Both play a role in explanations:
Deductive Explanations: These often take the form of deducing the phenomenon from a law or principle. For instance, you explain why a thrown ball follows a parabola by deducing it from the laws of gravity and motion. This is very bottom-up: start from fundamental laws (general) and logically entail the specific result. The Deductive-Nomological model of explanation (proposed by philosophers like Carl Hempel) formalized this idea: something is explained if you can show it’s a logical consequence of general laws plus initial conditions. This is rigorous in a strict sense – it’s like solving a puzzle with no gaps. However, it requires those general laws to be accepted and understood by the audience. If someone doesn’t know Newton’s laws, you might have to explain those first (which again you’d do bottom-up from even more basic concepts or experiments). Deductive explanations are powerful because they’re guaranteed by logic: if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. But they can feel a bit abstract if one doesn’t see the intuitive connection.
Inductive Explanations: These might look like giving a pattern or experience-based reasoning. For example, to explain why you think a certain stock price will fall, you might cite several past instances or indicators (specifics) that all suggest a trend, and from those infer the general outcome that the price will drop. This is less certain (induction can lead to wrong conclusions if the examples aren’t representative), but it’s often how humans form hypotheses and learn. In explanation, induction is often used to build up a concept as we did with virtue: listing examples and then abstracting. It’s a bottom-up in the sense of starting with specifics. The risk is if one induces incorrectly or too hastily (“swans are white, because all swans I’ve seen are white” – which fails when a black swan appears). Rigorous explanation uses induction carefully – typically to illustrate rather than to prove.
Abductive Reasoning: Though not explicitly asked, it’s worth a quick mention: abduction is inference to the best explanation – basically an educated guess of the cause. For instance, seeing a wet street and abducing that it rained. Good explanations often involve an abductive step: we pick the most plausible cause (using parsimony, etc.). But once we pick it, we then try to support it with either deductive details or inductive evidence.
In practice, a rigorous explanation might use a blend: for instance, induce a general principle from some data (to get the principle itself), then deduce specific consequences of that principle to show it indeed accounts for the phenomenon. The explanation will be rigorous if at each step the reasoning is valid and the assumptions are well-founded or minimal. Logical fallacies are the enemy of rigor. Circular reasoning is one such fallacy, as we’ve seen. Others include non sequiturs (explanation doesn’t actually follow), post hoc ergo propter hoc (assuming causation from sequence without justification), etc. A rigorous explanation avoids these – it aims for a clear logical connection from premises to conclusion. Deductive reasoning gives the strongest connection, so where possible, frame parts of your explanation deductively (e.g. because A and B, therefore C). Inductive parts should be signaled as such (e.g. “by analogy” or “for example”), and not over-claimed as proof. To summarize, logic in explanation is about structure: ensure the explanation doesn’t assume the conclusion (avoiding petitio principii) and that it provides a valid argument from known/accepted things to the thing explained. If you feel an explanation is rigorous, you often can map it out like a little logical argument: this, and this, and this, hence (explained thing). If you try that and realize the explained thing was also hidden in the “this” statements, you’ve got a circular argument. Good logical discipline prevents that.
Psychological Factors: Cognitive Load and Understanding
From psychology and education research, a few concepts shed light on why certain explanations resonate:
Cognitive Load Theory: As mentioned, Cognitive Load Theory tells us that people have limited working memory for new information. If an explanation is too top-down, it might introduce multiple new abstract ideas at once, overloading the learner. A bottom-up explanation, by introducing one concept at a time and building on what the learner already knows, reduces intrinsic load (difficulty) and extraneous load (unnecessary complexity). It’s basically an instructional design reflection of “bottom-up is clearer.” For example, a teacher faced with explaining a complex concept will often use scaffolding: start with a familiar example, then add a layer, then another, which is exactly bottom-up. If they just dumped the final equation or theory without buildup, students might memorize it (rote) but not truly understand – or worse, just tune out. So cognitive psychology supports structuring explanations from simple to complex.
Illusion of Explanatory Depth (IoED): We touched on this earlier – people often think they have a deep explanation in mind when they only have a shallow one. For instance, many people feel they understand everyday devices or phenomena until they’re asked how exactly it works, at which point they realize their “explanation” was more like a vague recollection of a description. This is why asking someone to explain something step-by-step is a great way to test understanding (the Feynman Technique of learning is based on this: if you can’t explain it simply, you might not really get it). IoED suggests that our minds are comfortable with top-down, familiarity-based “understanding” until pressed. A rigorous explanation dispels the illusion by articulating each link – often revealing that more learning or clarification is needed. It’s a bit humbling: when you try to explain and find yourself going in circles, you confront that illusion. The good news is, recognizing this is the first step to genuine understanding. It encourages a mindset of curiosity and humility: let’s not assume we know – let’s try to explain it properly, and fill in the gaps we discover.
Satisfaction and Trust: Psychologically, a well-structured explanation just feels more satisfying. Studies even with children have shown that even young kids prefer explanations that give a mechanism over ones that are circular. As adults, we’re more likely to trust and remember an explanation that makes sense down to the details. If someone always gives you hand-wavy or circular answers, you might suspect they’re hiding something or don’t know the answer. In contrast, someone who can break something down shows mastery, which earns credibility. Of course, there’s an element of knowing your audience: a completely rigorous, detailed explanation might be overkill if someone just wants a quick intuition. There’s a balance between rigor and relevance in communication.
Bringing these formal threads together: Philosophy of explanation emphasizes soundness and depth, logic ensures structure and non-circularity, and psychology ensures clarity and digestibility. A truly rigorous explanation hits all these notes: logically valid, deep but not needlessly convoluted, and accessible enough to genuinely enlighten the audience rather than confuse.
5. Implications for Communication & Science
Understanding what makes an explanation rigorous isn’t just an academic exercise – it has real implications in how we communicate ideas, teach and learn, and even how we advance scientific knowledge.
Science and Research: The progress of science often depends on finding non-circular explanations for natural phenomena. History is rife with moments where a mere label was not enough, and a deeper mechanism had to be uncovered. For instance, for a long time scientists described combustion by saying it released “phlogiston” – a hypothetical substance – which was a bit of a circular placeholder. It took Lavoisier’s work to explain combustion in terms of oxygen chemical reactions, a bottom-up explanation that replaced phlogiston theory and advanced chemistry. In modern science, researchers strive to reduce higher-level theories to more fundamental ones (like unifying forces in physics or explaining psychological phenomena in terms of neuroscience). When they succeed, science moves forward – when they fail or get stuck with circular reasoning, it signals a gap in understanding. Also, the principle of parsimony guides scientists to favor theories that elegantly explain data without requiring ad hoc assumptions. In practical terms, scientists design experiments to test the lower-level mechanisms, not just to re-verify what we already labeled. A rigorous explanatory framework in science also enables interdisciplinary connection: for example, a biologist explaining an ecosystem in terms of species interactions (bottom-up) might connect to a chemist explaining soil nutrients, etc. Each layer can trust the other if each is well-grounded. If one layer was just “magic happens here,” the chain breaks. Thus, rigorous explanations are like strong links that allow knowledge to build cumulatively. As a scientist, it’s crucial to watch out for explanations that merely restate data (e.g. “genes cause trait X because they are genes for X” – you’d need to find what that gene actually does). The demand for rigor drives further investigation.
Education and Teaching: For teachers and communicators, the takeaway is to build explanations step by step and avoid assuming knowledge that students don’t have. This means checking your definitions for circularity. It’s surprisingly easy to inadvertently define a term with itself (even seasoned dictionary editors have to avoid it). Teachers often use what’s called a defining vocabulary – a limited set of simpler words to define new words, precisely to avoid circular definitions. When introducing a new concept, tie it to something already understood or give a concrete example. If a student looks confused, sometimes it’s because the explanation was too abstract or self-referential. Encouraging students to ask the classic toddler question “why?” repeatedly can train them (and you) to drill down to fundamentals. Of course, there’s a practical limit in a classroom – you can’t always go down the rabbit hole to quantum physics for every high school physics question. But you can be honest about what you’re taking as given and perhaps point to where those givens come from. Good teaching also addresses misconceptions often born of half-baked explanations. A lot of misconceptions in science come from taking a shortcut that later becomes a mental block. For example, younger students might be told “you can’t see the Moon in daytime because the Sun is too bright” – which is not strictly true (the Moon is often visible in daytime). A rigorous explanation of moon visibility would involve brightness and contrasts and angles; simplifying it wrongly can plant a misconception. Thus, rigor (appropriate to level) matters to ensure students aren’t learning something they’ll have to unlearn later.
Effective Communication: Whether in writing, public speaking, or everyday arguments, the principles of rigorous explanation can make your points more convincing. If you present a clear cause-and-effect chain, anticipate and avoid circular logic, and use analogies (bottom-up examples) that resonate, your audience will follow you better. In contrast, if people sense circular reasoning or a hand-wave, they may feel unconvinced or even manipulated. For instance, in persuasive writing or debate, an opponent will jump on any begging the question fallacy – if your reasoning assumes your conclusion, it’s not persuasive. Being rigorous means your explanations stand on their own merit. This fosters trust and credibility. It’s especially important when explaining complex or controversial topics to the public. Take vaccine science as an example: simply saying “the vaccine works because it’s effective” won’t sway skeptics; you need to explain how it stimulates the immune system (in accessible terms) and provide evidence – a mini bottom-up explanation that is transparent and therefore more trustworthy. In an age of misinformation, the ability to communicate clearly without circular jargon is a superpower. It helps audiences distinguish between real expertise and bluffing. We’ve all heard politicians or spokespeople give answers that circle around without saying much; contrast that with someone who straightforwardly explains the reasoning behind a policy – even if we disagree, we appreciate the clarity.
Building Knowledge and Lifelong Learning: For the individual learner or professional, remembering to seek bottom-up understanding can guide self-study. If you’re reading about a concept and the explanation feels like it’s using a lot of unexplained buzzwords, don’t be satisfied – dig deeper. Find sources that break it down. Sometimes textbooks or articles inadvertently assume knowledge (a top-down approach) and you might have to flip back to earlier chapters or foundational texts. Don’t be afraid to ask “But why?” at each step when learning something new. This is essentially performing a Socratic dialogue with yourself or the material to ensure you’re not just memorizing a definition that defines itself. It can be effortful, but it’s rewarding. By doing so, you avoid the illusion of explanatory depth for yourself – you truly own the knowledge. Moreover, this mindset makes you a better problem-solver: you can apply concepts in new contexts because you understand how they arise, not just that they exist.
Clarity vs. Accuracy: One might wonder, does simplifying to avoid circularity sometimes sacrifice nuance? It’s possible to err by oversimplifying – for example, using a too-simple bottom-up explanation that isn’t quite accurate. Rigor means both clear and correct. So one must be careful: in avoiding a confusing term, don’t replace it with a wrong analogy. For instance, explaining electricity flow as “like water” is a helpful bottom-up analogy, but it has limits and can introduce misconceptions if taken too literally. The balance comes from knowing your audience’s level and gradually refining the explanation. Start simple to give the gist (without being circular), then add complexity as needed for accuracy. Rigor doesn’t mean dumping all complexity at once; it means whatever you do say is logically sound and not misleading.
In closing, applying these principles of rigorous explanation creates a virtuous cycle: communicators convey ideas more effectively, learners build real understanding, and knowledge gets refined rather than obscured. Whether you’re writing a blog post (like this one!), teaching a class, or just satisfying your own curiosity, remembering to derive concepts from lower levels and avoid circularity will serve you well. As Einstein (or Feynman, depending on the attribution) famously said, “If you can’t explain it simply, you don’t understand it well enough.” This speaks to deriving an explanation from basics – making it simple but not simpler than appropriate. And as we’ve explored, “simply” doesn’t mean superficially or circularly – it means clearly, from the ground up. Conclusion:
A rigorous explanation is one that truly explains – it sheds light on the unknown by using the known. It takes apart mysteries into understandable pieces and ensures those pieces don’t secretly smuggle the mystery back in. By contrasting bottom-up and top-down approaches, we saw that building upward from foundations often provides the firmest understanding. We examined how circular reasoning and redundant definitions fail to move us forward, whereas well-structured explanations traverse from cause to effect or part to whole without looping back. Across science, philosophy, education, and daily life, the best explanations share a common DNA: they derive, they clarify, and they enlighten. They might require more effort to formulate – digging one level deeper, choosing the right analogies, verifying logical consistency – but they pay off by enriching our comprehension and ability to think critically. In a world overloaded with information and sometimes “because I said so” answers, the art of rigorous explanation is more important than ever. It empowers us to cut through confusion, learn efficiently, and communicate effectively. Next time you encounter an explanation, apply a little pressure: does it stand up to “Why? How?” questions, or does it collapse into circularity? And when you explain something – whether to a friend, a child, or an audience of thousands – try anchoring it in solid ground and building up. Your explanation will be more robust, and your listeners will thank you for actually answering the question, not just rephrasing it. In essence, good explanations don’t go in circles – they build upward, turning unknowns into knowables one step at a time.

1. The Hierarchy of Concepts: What Makes Something Higher or Lower Level?
A lower-level concept is more fundamental, atomic, or directly observable, while a higher-level concept emerges from multiple lower-level ones or represents an abstraction over many instances.
Key Criteria for a Concept’s Level
To determine if a concept is higher-level or lower-level, consider:
Dependency:
If concept B depends on understanding concept A, then A is lower-level than B.
Example: In physics, velocity depends on position changing over time. Therefore, position is lower-level than velocity.
Likewise, sentences depend on words, making words lower-level than sentences.
Reductionism:
Can the concept be explained in terms of simpler, smaller components?
If yes, then those components are lower-level.
Example: A car is made up of an engine, wheels, and transmission. These components are lower-level because you can explain a car in terms of them.
