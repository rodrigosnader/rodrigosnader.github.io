Here’s a clean article version, same language, same line of thought.
Free Will Without the Ghost in the Machine
The standard argument against free will usually goes like this:
everything about your brain is the result of prior causes, so any decision you make is just the inevitable consequence of the universe unfolding. Therefore, you don’t have free will.
This argument sounds serious, but it rests on a quiet confusion about what “you” refers to.

If you sum up all the events that shaped your brain—your biology, memories, experiences, habits, values—that is what you are. There is no extra “you” sitting on top of those influences, watching them happen. Without that history, there would be no subject to even ask the question of free will.

So asking “do you have free will?” is not the same as asking “could your brain state be different right now?”
The answer to the second question is obviously no. Given the exact state of the universe, your brain could not be different. Full stop.

But that fact is irrelevant.

The brain state you have right now is you. When that brain evaluates reasons and decides to go right instead of left, that decision is not something that merely happened to you. It is what you did. Saying “it was determined” does not mean “it wasn’t yours.” It means it was determined by you.

The mistake appears most clearly in the libertarian idea of free will, which says that even if all conditions were identical, you still could have chosen otherwise. But what are those “identical conditions” supposed to include?

If they include the entire universe—including your brain, your reasons, your character—then the demand becomes incoherent. It amounts to asking:

Could you have decided to go left, even though you decided to go right, with everything (including you) exactly the same?
That’s not a deep metaphysical requirement. It’s a contradiction. It quietly assumes two versions of you: one fully specified by the universe, and another hidden chooser that can override it without being different in any way.
Once you stop smuggling in that second self, the problem dissolves. There is no gap between “the causal process” and “the agent.” You are not a static object trapped inside causality. You are the causal process. You are the change, the deliberation, and the response to influence.

Under this view, free will doesn’t mean “uncaused choice.” It means that choices flow from your internal reasons, values, and deliberation rather than from coercion or randomness. Determinism doesn’t erase agency; it explains what agency is made of.

The question “could you have done otherwise?” only makes sense counterfactually:
if your reasons were different, you would have acted differently.
But demanding a different outcome with no difference anywhere—including in you—is not freedom. It’s a category error.

Once “you” are fully included in the universe, free will stops being mysterious—and libertarian free will stops being a meaningful thing to ask for.

Here’s a fuller, self-contained article that puts everything on the table, using the same language and framing we used in the discussion.
Free Will Once “You” Are Put Back Into the Universe
The classic argument against free will goes roughly like this:
your brain is the product of genetics, environment, prior experiences, and physical laws. Given the exact state of the universe at any moment, the next state is fixed. Therefore, any decision you make was inevitable. Therefore, you have no free will.
This argument feels compelling, but it depends on a subtle confusion about what “you” are supposed to be.

If you take all the events that shaped your brain—biology, memories, learning, trauma, culture, habits, values—that total is precisely what you are. There is no extra self floating above those influences. Without them, there would be no “you” to even ask the question.

Once that’s clear, an important distinction appears.

Asking “do you have free will?” is not the same as asking “could your brain state be different right now?”
The answer to the second question is obviously no. If the universe were identical down to the last detail, your brain state could not be different. Period.

But that fact doesn’t undermine agency. The brain state you have right now is you. When that system evaluates reasons and decides to go right instead of left, that decision is not something that merely happened to you. It is what you did. Determinism doesn’t mean “not your choice.” It means “your choice has causes”—namely, you.

This position is called compatibilism.

The Compatibilist Model
Compatibilism holds that free will and determinism are not in conflict. Free will does not require freedom from causation; it requires the right kind of causation.
Under this model:

You act freely when your actions flow from your own reasons, values, and deliberation.
Coercion, manipulation, or compulsion undermine freedom because they bypass or override that internal process.
The fact that the internal process itself has causes does not negate agency—it constitutes it.
Compatibilism treats “you” as identical with the decision-making process, not as something separate from it.
The Libertarian Model
Libertarian free will rejects this. Libertarians insist that genuine free will requires the ability to have done otherwise under identical conditions.
That phrase is doing a lot of work.

By “identical conditions,” libertarians typically mean:

the entire past state of the universe,
the laws of nature,
your brain state,
your character,
your reasons and desires.
All of it fixed.
And yet, they still want it to be true that you could have chosen differently.

This is where the problem appears.

If the entire universe is identical, then you are identical.
If you are identical, then your deliberation is identical.
If the deliberation is identical, then the outcome is identical.

So what, exactly, is being varied?

Libertarian free will quietly assumes a second version of you:

a chooser behind the brain,
a self not fully captured by the physical or functional description,
something that can generate a different outcome without being different in any describable way.
Once you explicitly include you in the conditions, the libertarian question collapses into something like:
“Would you be able to decide to go left, even though you decided to go right, with everything—including you—unchanged?”
That’s not a deep metaphysical demand. It’s a malformed question. It asks for difference without difference, agency without an agent distinct from the agent.
What Libertarians Are Really Asking
When stripped of the confusion, the libertarian intuition usually amounts to one of two things:
A discomfort with being fully explained.
A desire for a self that is not identical to any physical or causal process.
But once you reject that dualism and say “you just are the process,” the demand for libertarian freedom stops making sense. There is no place left for the extra freedom to live.
The only coherent sense in which “you could have done otherwise” survives is counterfactual, not absolute:

if your reasons were different, you would have acted differently;
if your values were different, the outcome would differ.
That is perfectly compatible with determinism and perfectly sufficient for responsibility, deliberation, regret, learning, and moral evaluation.
The Core Insight
The error in the standard anti–free will argument is the same error in the libertarian response: both subtract “you” from the universe and then wonder where agency went.
Once you are fully included in the causal story, free will stops being mysterious. You are not a static thing trapped inside a deterministic machine. You are the machine, the change, the influence, and the response to influence.

Determinism doesn’t eliminate free will.
It explains what free will is made of.

Libertarian free will doesn’t fail because the universe is deterministic.
It fails because, once “you” are properly defined, the question it asks no longer forms a coherent thought.
