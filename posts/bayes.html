The one thing Bayes actually says

2026 · Apr 12 · 8 min read · Essay

You just tested positive for a disease. The test is "95% accurate." How worried should you be?

Most people's gut says "95% worried." Mine certainly did. The honest answer is usually much lower, and the way to get there is called Bayes' rule.

If you've ever tried to look up what Bayes' rule is, you know the shape of it: an equation with three ingredients (prior, likelihood, evidence) that you're supposed to hold in your head and keep straight. I came away the first few times with a vague sense that Bayes was important and no real idea how to use it.

Here's where I think the textbook might fail. It decomposes the rule into three parts because the algebra has three parts. Then it tries to give each part a name and a role, which bleed into each other, and the student ends up with an equation that feels like separate ingredients being whisked around for no clear reason.

I spent an afternoon arguing with a language model until the whole thing made sense, boiled down to one question:

The question

Is this evidence happening because my belief is true, or is it happening anyway?

Or, for this specific case: is this positive test showing up because I'm actually sick — or does it read positive for healthy people anyway?

That's the entire rule. Once you have the question, everything else — prior, likelihood, evidence — starts to make sense. But I'm guessing that sentence doesn't land yet. It only clicks once you watch it play out on something real. Let's walk through the positive test slowly.

Your positive test, slowly

Let's suppose a disease like COVID affects 1% of the population. And let's say the test catches sick people 95% of the time (tests are not perfect). So for every 100 people, we'd have 0.95 caught as sick, on average — that's the because case (positives happening because the person is actually sick). The other 99 are healthy.

Let's also imagine that 5% of these healthy people get false positives (again, tests are not perfect!) — that gives us another 4.95 positives from healthy people. That's the anyway case (positives that happen anyway, even without anyone being sick).

Total positives in a group of 100: about 5.9.

Notice how out of those 5.9 positives, only 0.95 came from the because group. The other 4.95 are the anyway group — false alarms. So even if your own test shows positive, your chance of really being sick is 0.95 out of 5.9 — roughly 16%. Not 95%. There are just so many more healthy people to be wrong about than sick people to be right about, and the false positives swamp the real ones.

In other words: if your test shows positive, there are really only two ways it could have happened. It might be positive because you're actually sick. Or it might be showing positive anyway, the kind of false alarm the test throws on healthy people too. Weighing both together is what gives you your real chance of being sick.

That's Bayes in one picture: the orange fraction is your answer, and it only gets big when the disease itself is common enough to outweigh the false alarms.

Where the textbook names fit

If you go look up Bayes after this, you'll run into the same three ingredients — prior, likelihood, evidence — and now they map onto something concrete:

  1. Prior — the 1-in-100 you started with. What you knew before the test.
  2. Likelihood — the 95%. How often the signal shows up when your guess is right.
  3. Evidence — the 5.9%. How often the signal shows up at all.

The formulas wrap those three numbers into a fraction and spit out the 16% — just a compact way to write the same thing.

Why this matters outside of tests

The same question applies outside of medical tests. A suspicious email in your inbox: how often do real phishing attempts look like this, versus how often do legitimate messages happen to sound like this? A candidate with an impressive résumé: how often do great engineers have résumés like that, versus how often do mediocre ones polish theirs to look the same? A scientific result with a low p-value: how often does a real effect produce this, versus how often does chance alone?

Bayes is just the habit of always asking both halves instead of just the first. Most of the bad inference in the world — hiring mistakes, medical panic, conspiracy theories that "explain everything" — comes from forgetting to ask the second half. The signal looks strong, and you don't check how strong the signal is anyway.

So next time someone tells you they saw a flying saucer, ask:

  1. How often do reports like this end up having an ordinary explanation — a natural event, a trick of the light, a drifted memory?
  2. How many flying saucers have ever been verified?

The first number is big. The second is (probably) zero. Any single report is overwhelmingly anyway, not because.

This is the same cognitive blind spot I poked at in the skepticism post, from a different angle. False statements vastly outnumber true ones; weak evidence for something is still mostly evidence for nothing, because "nothing" is so much larger.

The whole rule, one more time

Bayes' rule is the habit of asking, for any piece of evidence:

How much of the evidence happens because my belief is true, versus how much would happen anyway?

That's the whole rule. The formulas and the three-part jargon are all ways of writing that one comparison down precisely. Once you have the habit, the rest is just notation.
