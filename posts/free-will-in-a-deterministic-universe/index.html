Free will in a deterministic universe

2026 · Aug 19 · 7 min read · Philosophy

Most people, when told the universe is fully deterministic and consciousness is purely physical, conclude that free will is an illusion.

The argument goes like this: every atom follows fixed laws, every thought is just neurons firing on a preset schedule, and "you," choosing to raise your hand, was already written into the physics at the Big Bang. No choice. No agency. No free will.

In other words:

Your brain is the product of genetics, environment, prior experiences, and physical laws. Given the exact state of the universe at any moment, the next state is fixed. Therefore, any decision you make was inevitable.

If we treat free will as the ability to make decisions — to choose one path over another — the argument is coherent and feels hard to argue with. But I think it might be answering the wrong question.

Two Questions

Buried inside the claim that determinism settles the question of agency there are two separate questions that may look the same at first:

  1. Do you have free will?
  2. Could your brain state be different right now?

The second question answers itself. If the universe was identical in every detail — down to each particle — your brain state could not differ. This is where the critique of free will enters with the premise that you didn't decide. Your brain did.

Neuroscience adds empirical weight here. For example, in Libet's 1983 experiments, EEG detected neural activity preparing a wrist movement roughly 550ms before participants consciously chose to act. This could mean that you don't choose your actions. You realize them.

Second question is done. But let's see what that means for the first one, because there might be some language nuance we're missing there.

Description Levels

You, as a human being, are a collection of atoms — a small part of the entire deterministic system we call the universe. At the most fundamental level, the question "do you have choices?" isn't answered yes or no. It's a malformed question. The concepts it relies on — "you" and "choice" — don't exist there.

But there's a reason we use those words. Treating a particular pattern of atoms that behaves coherently and self-organizing as a "person" is enormously useful. There are no chairs at the level of particles. Yet "this chair is made of wood" is a true statement. Humans classify four-legged seats as chairs because the categorization lets us predict and explain properties and behaviors of it in ways that tracking atoms can't.

Now, notice how asking a question at the wrong level of description makes some answers undefined. A single molecule has a velocity, but asking for its temperature doesn't really make sense — it's a property that only appears once you have enough particles together.

Here's another example: ask whether a single frame of a film contains a plot twist. The answer is not even "no". Nothing in a frame reverses, nothing in it moves. Films have plot twists, frames don't.

This seems to be the shape of the argument against free will: it takes a question posed at the level of persons — "did you decide?" — and answers it at the level of particles.

Plot twists are properties of frames in sequence in the same way that decisions are properties of brain-states in sequence. And once you see that, the two questions stated above mean entirely different things.

The impression of the two questions being the same starts from treating you as your brain, which may not be the case.

Properly Defining You

There are a few ways to understand what "you" refers to in the free-will context, so let's check each case.

Case 1 — The still-frame

"You" means a frozen snapshot of your brain at one instant — a static configuration of particles.

The assumption here feels problematic because choosing is an action (thus, a verb), something that happens over time. To choose, one needs to evaluate options. To evaluate, one needs to understand, etc. The act of deciding, by itself, is a process.

A snapshot of neurons is as alive as a rock. It doesn't see, move, feel, or remember — each of these is something the brain does over time — and no one's asking whether a rock decides (maybe panpsychists are, but leave this conversation for another time!).

So if "you" means a snapshot, the free will question dissolves simply because a frozen state is not even alive. And at that point we're not talking about free will anymore.

Case 2 — The observer

Here, "you" means only the conscious self: the aware, deliberating part of the brain that Libet was measuring. On this view, the unconscious brain does the processing first, then consciousness receives the result. The brain decides and you become aware of the decision.

But we normally treat plenty of unconscious processes as parts of ourselves:

  • our memories while they're not being actively recalled;
  • automated day to day habits;
  • much of our preferences, impulses, and emotional reactions;

That puts us in a strange position: why would we claim that all of those are part of what we are, but suddenly not assume that anymore for the free-will question?

If we define "you" as only the contents of conscious awareness, we have to accept a surprisingly narrow conclusion: your habits are not you, your memories are not you, and so on. And that seems to put the boundary in the wrong place.

Plus, becoming conscious is also a process. And it's easy to accept that you'd only become aware once the process has run its course.

Case 3 — The entire system

This is where I land, at least for now: "you" means the full physical system in operation — the whole brain running its processes across time, memory and deliberation and history and all.

  • Your current brain state → you
  • Your previous and future brain states → you
  • The process that unfolds over time to reach a decision → you
  • External influences and inputs (or at least how your brain receives them) → somehow part of you

If a friend's advice changed how you weigh a choice, that advice didn't happen to you from outside. It became part of the system that decides.

The determinism that was supposed to rob you of agency runs through you, not around you. A caused decision is still yours, and being the system those causes produced is exactly what it is to be you.

What It Means to Decide

Imagine a decision-making system governed by internal rules A, B, and C. It receives external inputs, processes them according to its rules, its internal state, and its history, and produces a decision.

If the system is deterministic, then given exactly the same inputs and the same internal state, it will always produce the same decision.

Does that mean it has no capacity to decide? Well, deciding is precisely what the system does: process information according to internal criteria and produce an action. The fact that the outcome is determined does not dissolve the decision-making process. In other words:

Determinism means that the outcome of a decision-making system is settled by the process that system goes through, not that the system doesn't decide. Going through the process is precisely what deciding is.

What such system cannot do is, given the same inputs, under exactly the same internal conditions, produce a different outcome. That would require the system to be a different system.

This is where the argument against free will becomes contradictory. To count as free, the system is being required to be what it isn't.

Compatibilist Free Will

This view has a name: compatibilism — the position that free will and determinism are compatible and a causal process is what free will consists of.

You act freely when your actions flow from your own reasons, values, and deliberation. Freedom isn't about uncaused choice; it's about whether the causes that produce your action are yours.
