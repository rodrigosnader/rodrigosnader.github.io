Why truth is narrow

2026 · Apr 15 · 8 min read · Essay

Here's a question: are there more possible true statements about the world, or more possible false ones?

Sit with it for a second. It isn't a trick — the answer is the obvious one — but the size of the gap is not obvious, and the implications are even less so.

"The cup of coffee on my desk is about two-thirds full." There's roughly one true way to say that. There are, however, unbounded ways to miss. "The cup is full." "The cup is kind of empty." "The mug is two-thirds full." "There's a good amount of coffee there." "The cup is a lemon." Each of those is a possible sentence. And each of them is false, imprecise, or opinionated.

For any single fact in the world, there's a thin sliver of statements that match it and a continent of statements that don't. The gap is huge enough that "ratio" stops feeling like the right word for it. Truth is narrow; everything else is everywhere.

The asymmetry

Just due to the nature of language, for every claim that accurately describes the world there are unbounded variations that distort, misremember, invent, or simply miss. Reality has the one narrow version. The space of false versions is the rest of language.

Default-believing a claim, then, is not a neutral stance. It's a loaded one, tilted toward falsehood by the same gap. If you walk around accepting things that arrive at your ears unless something trips your suspicion, the ambient statistics of the universe aren't on your side. The space of false versions so dwarfs the space of true ones that even a heavy filter — believe only one in a hundred claims that reach you — can still leave you mostly wrong, depending on which one you keep.

The plausibility filter is a weak filter

Of course most claims you encounter aren't generated at random. They've been filtered. They come from friends, writers, journalists, books, search results. The filter has done a lot of work before the claim ever reaches you.

But the filter is plausibility, and plausibility is a weaker bar than it sounds. Plausibility is what makes a claim feel like the kind of thing that could be true. It does not require the claim to actually match reality. It only requires the claim to sound like one of the kinds of things that sometimes match reality. That bar is much lower than people give it credit for.

Skepticism is the second layer — the one that asks, after the plausibility filter has done its work, is it actually true? And the reason this layer keeps getting left out of people's workflows is that it's slow, effortful, and unrewarding in the moment. It takes time to check. It takes time to doubt the thing you already half-believe. Most of us skip it unless something is at stake.

The "open mind" trap

There's a move people make in response, and I keep running into it. The move is to claim that being open to everything is a virtue, and that skepticism is a closed-minded defensive crouch. It usually arrives in the shape of: "I keep an open mind. You never know. There's always a possibility."

I don't want to attack the impulse, because the impulse is good. There's a real failure mode of being so locked into your priors that you dismiss surprising evidence, and being open to that evidence is a virtue. But "open to evidence" is not the same as "willing to believe whatever's in the air." Evidence has an internal structure — it can be checked, traced, reproduced, compared against predictions. Claims that arrive without evidence have no such structure. They are sentences. Being more inclined to believe than not in a sentence that just showed up is not open-mindedness; it is leaving the front door unlocked and calling it hospitality.

The phrase keep an open mind has become a socially polite substitute for I don't want to do the work of checking.

One question to flip

Here's the small habit I've been trying to train in myself.

When I read a claim, the question I instinctively ask is: could this be true? And I can almost always answer yes. Sure, it could be true. It's plausible. It's consistent with what I already believe. Fine. Moving on.

The flip

The right question isn't could this be true? — it's could this be false? The first lets almost everything through. The second makes you imagine the world in which the claim is wrong, and check whether you actually have any reason to rule that world out.

Most of the time, when I flip the question, I discover I had no reason to rule out the false version. I was accepting the claim because it fit a story I already half-believed, not because I had any positive evidence for it. That's the failure mode the "could this be true?" default hides.

Why this is worse now than it used to be

The asymmetry has gotten worse in practical terms over the last few years, for several reasons — but one is relatively new, special, and everywhere at once: AI-generated content.

The cost of producing plausible-sounding claims has dropped to almost nothing. A language model can generate a thousand coherent, confident claims a minute, and the generator isn't optimizing for truth — it's optimizing for sounding like the kind of thing that's true. Sometimes those coincide. Often they don't. The flow of plausible claims reaching you is higher than it's ever been, and the part of you that filters by "does this sound true?" is being fed inputs designed to pass that filter.

I'm not really arguing against AI-generated content. If anything, it's just forcing us to do the homework we should always have been doing — with news, with claims, with anything that arrives wrapped in a confident sentence. The homework didn't start mattering now. It only started being unavoidable now.

Truth is narrow. Falsehood is everywhere. The smallest move you can make is to stop asking could this be true? of claims you encounter and start asking could this be false?
