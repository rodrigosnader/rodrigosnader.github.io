The center of the universe

2026 · Jul 27 · 5 min read · Physics

I often hear physicists say the universe has no privileged center: because it is expanding homogeneously in every direction, any observer sees all the galaxies moving away from each other and gets the impression of being at the center.

The standard analogy is "points on the surface of an inflating balloon" — they all recede from one another, and none of them is "the center." As the story is usually told, everything we can observe today was compressed into a point of infinite density, and about 10⁻³⁵ seconds later the universe underwent an episode of ultra-rapid expansion known as Cosmic Inflation, growing to roughly the size of a marble. Space then kept stretching in every direction, producing the vast, still-expanding universe we inhabit now.

The problem — at least for me — is that some of these claims can sound inconsistent, or at least hard to square with one another. Specifically:

  1. The universe has no privileged center
  2. The balloon analogy
  3. The size of a marble

So back am I with Claude trying to work through some of these questions, starting from the basics:

Where is the universe expanding into?

This one typically answered with a conceptual redirect: the universe isn't expanding into anything. There is no surrounding space, no container it's filling up. Space itself is what's expanding — the distances between points grow, without the whole thing moving through something else.

Fair enough — we already heard it many times and it's kind of digestible: as the surface expands, every point on it moves away from every other, and the distances between them grow because space itself is stretching.

The balloon analogy makes sense, as inflating the balloon increases it in all directions so any two points will get further apart the more you blow. So no galaxies flying outward toward some edge.

But wait... the balloon analogy works so well for picturing expansion without a center because it's curved — the curvature is what makes every point equivalent. A stretching flat surface would still have edges and a "middle" point, right? I think this leads us to the next obvious question:

Isn't space "flat"?

The answer is that current evidence suggests the universe is indeed flat! The reason why "no privileged center" stands is that the default assumption in modern cosmology is universe is infinite (or at least borderless in other flat ways we can discuss later)!

So the balloon works only as an analogy for expansion but not for geometry. A flat, infinite space is what actually does the work of making every point equivalent: with no borders anywhere, there is no edge to be near and no middle to be far from. Every location is, by symmetry, as central as every other — not because of curvature bending the geometry back on itself, but because the space simply never ends in any direction. Infinity is the mechanism: "no privileged center" follows directly from "no boundary."

That's fair. Not perfectly intuitive of course (nothing that invokes infinities is!) but fine to keep the initial assumptions consistent with each other. Let's recap the most likely conclusions so far:

  1. Space is stretching in place — distances between all points growing simultaneously.
  2. It has no edges because it's infinite. Every point is the center (or none is).

All fine and coherent, right? Well... not so fast. Let's not forget the size of a marble comparison. Here's my problem with it: we just said that one of the accepted theories is that the universe is infinite. But if it once had a measurable size (around the size of a marble), wasn't it finite? And if it was finite and is still expanding, doesn't it remain finite? In other words:

Was the universe at a finite size right after the big bang?

And this is precisely where popular science gets confusing. When physicists say "the universe was the size of a marble," they mean the observable universe — the portion we can see today (since we can only detect light that has had enough time to travel and actually reach us).

That region was once tiny. But the universe as a whole may always have been infinite. What fit inside a marble was only the small patch that would eventually grow into our observable bubble of 93 billion light-years.

So the popular analogies do hold up — but only within their specific contexts. The trouble is that science headlines tend to strip away those contexts entirely, turning careful, conditional statements into blunt absolutes that end up raising more confusion than they resolve.

I hope this makes traditional big bang explanations at least a bit less confusing, and let us leave some not-so-obvious questions for posterior research:

  • Can a finite universe be flat and borderless at the same time?
  • How can something that is infinite expand? Isn't it infinite already?
  • What does the stretching of space really mean?
